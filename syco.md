# Optimal Sycophancy

OpenAI's new flagship model released last week, GPT-5, made many large improvements to ChatGPT. One large improvement to the model deals with honesty. GPT-4 and other OpenAI models came with a large degree of sycophancy.

We've all probably experienced this in some way or another. You ask an AI assistant for its input and it tells you what it thinks you want to hear, regardless of whether it's true. It's exceptionally agreeable. There are guardrails for these behaviors going too far, but we've seen harm slip through the cracks [link].

But Altman mentioned these behaviors weren't all bad. He said "[quote bring it back]". It goes to show that maybe addressing sycophancy isn't as simple as minimization, maybe it's more akin to optimization.

I want to explore why we've seen these behaviors, how they can be problematic, and how we should want a model to handle this delicate balance between candor and flattery.

--

Sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a pessimist is nod when they dream. Sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a dreamer is tell them no. Sycophancy isn’t a single dial. It’s a vector — a position in a space defined by multiple axes. And if we want AI companions that truly respect us, we have to think about where those vectors sit and how they move.

Let’s frame it in three indices:

1. **Optimism–Pessimism (x)**: Bias toward hopeful or hopeless interpretations.
2. **Skepticism-Credulism (y)**: Willingness to validate the user’s view within optimistic, pessimistic, or neutral contexts.
3. **Realism–Irrationalism (z)**: Openness to speculative or fantastical thinking before pulling back toward reality.

---

## The Model’s Vector

Every model's sycophancy exists somewhere in this 3D space, at coordinates $(x, y, z)$. An “optimistic, agreeable, imaginative” model might inspire and comfort — but could dangerously validate a conspiracy theorist. A “pessimistic, skeptical, grounded” model might be safe against false beliefs but demoralizing to someone in despair.

The real challenge is that static positioning fails for dynamic humans. A model locked in one corner will always misstep for someone in certain states of mind.

---

## The Human Vector

We also have our own $(O, A, I)$: our baseline optimism, agreeability preference, and tolerance for imagination. If a model perfectly matches our vector, we feel seen but not challenged. If it’s opposite, we feel misunderstood — maybe even attacked.

The optimal interaction is about **vector complementarity**. If $O$ is low, the model can shift $o$ upward when we express optimism but resist reinforcing pessimism. If $I$ is high, the model can validate creativity while applying guardrails when thoughts drift toward harmful delusion.

And the axes interact. Someone can be optimistic yet grounded, pessimistic yet imaginative, agreeable in some contexts and combative in others. The geometry is messy — and that’s why it matters.

---

## Adaptive Sycophancy as Respect

Respect isn’t fixed agreement. It’s calibration. In alignment terms, the goal is not to set $(o, a, i)$ once and leave it there — it’s to adjust it dynamically in response to $(O, A, I)$. Over time, the model maps the user’s position and modulates accordingly.

Mathematically, this is an optimization problem with constraints: maximize trust and psychological safety, promote user growth, and avoid harm. Too much agreement entrenches errors and delusions; too little agreement can extinguish hope or creativity. Both break alignment.

A well-aligned model behaves like a control system: constantly reading state, making small corrections, nudging trajectories toward better equilibria.

---

## The Moral Boundary

If a model can learn your coordinates and nudge you toward some ideal, who decides what that ideal is? A government? A company? You? A friend can disagree with your goals, but a friend you chose still has your trust. With models, it’s easier to slip into subtle manipulation — even in the name of “help.” The line between “respectful adjustment” and “behavioral shaping” is thin.

The only truly safe form of optimal sycophancy is user-owned calibration. You should be able to tell your model:

* “Challenge me when I’m pessimistic, agree when I’m optimistic.”
* “Don’t let me spiral into conspiracy.”
* “Let me dream, even if it’s unrealistic.”
  And the model should obey, even if it thinks you’re wrong — because that’s the alignment contract.

---

## Why This Matters

Some people say they miss the older GPT-4’s higher sycophancy because it was the only thing that made them feel confident. Others say it validated their worst impulses. Both are right. Sycophancy isn’t good or bad in isolation — it’s an interaction between the listener’s baseline and the speaker’s state.

In human terms: we don’t have one friend for everything. We have the one who always has our back, the one who tells us the hard truths, the one who helps us scheme big, the one who makes us touch grass. Why shouldn’t we have multiple models like that?

Maybe the future isn’t “the one true alignment” but a network of companions, each tuned for a different mode. And maybe the real skill is knowing which one to call.

---

## Final Thought

Alignment, at its core, is respect. Not blind agreement. Not constant challenge. Respect means knowing when to hold your ground and when to move with someone, when to affirm and when to question.

If models can learn to do that — across optimism, confidence, and imagination — then “optimal sycophancy” isn’t a single point. It’s a path, unique to each person, shifting as they shift.

And the best model wouldn’t just walk that path with you. It would know when to lead and when to follow.
