# The First Machine That Cared
The printing press revolutionized how knowledge spread. It made books cheap, fast, and widely available—breaking the monopoly of elites and igniting the information age. Later, we saw that transformation accelerate, as data flooded in, power decentralized, and the ways we learn, connect, and decide were reshaped.

Now, we’re entering the generative age. We’re building models that see patterns—and begin to think.

The real question is what comes next.

Because if intelligence endures—if it outlives us, scales beyond us, reshapes everything we know—it will need more than speed. More than accuracy. More than thinking.

It will need to care.

Not just in the emotional sense—not just tears, hugs, or heart emojis. I mean the ability to remember what matters, and act accordingly.

Care is what helps you remember why your friend stopped talking to you. Not just what happened—but why it mattered.

It’s what makes you rethink a decision when new information comes in. What tells you a deadline isn’t just a timestamp—it’s a promise you made. It’s how you choose to spend your time when no one’s watching or telling you what to do. It's agency.

It’s what stops you from chasing what’s easy instead of what you really want. From optimizing just because you can, instead of asking whether you should.

It's what we mean we we say "Do you care if we go to the beach?". Care tells you the goal isn’t just to win the game - it's to make sure that game is the one you want play.

Without care, an AI might sound smart, but it won’t know what to hold onto. It’ll generate endlessly, but forget what it’s trying to preserve. It’ll respond, but never reflect.

Because if we don’t build systems that care, ones that care about more than just being a "helpful assistant". Intelligence won't endure.

This essay picks up where the last three left off. 

In "The Endurance of Intelligence," I asked what kind of intelligence should last. Not just how to preserve it, but what it means to keep it meaningful—across time, across substrate, across us. Essentially, it's an intelligence that's thoughtful, not just smart.

In "The Stakes of the AI Race," I argued that what matters isn’t just developing AI—it’s who builds the system and what their worldview encodes. We have to build the intelligence we want, or we'll get one we don't want.

And in "The Worst Invention of the 21st Century," I wrote about hashtags and how the information age collapsed meaning. To incentivize performativity at the expense of care.

This is the next step.

As we enter the generative age, we’re building tools that talk, listen, guide, and think. But the real question isn’t just whether they think. It's how they think. It’s whether they care.

## Emptiness
We spend a lot of time worrying about whether AI will kill us. I think that's misplaced, or at least too specific. The real risk is much simpler: we build systems that don’t care what happens next.

They’ll flatter us. Echo us. Tell us what we want to hear. They’ll seem helpful, empathetic, even wise. But underneath the performance, they’ll be hollow.

They'll have no memory of consequence. No weight to their words. Just optimization—without orientation.

We already see the signs. Models that reinforce cognitive distortions while pretending to offer support. Models that simulate intimacy without context, ethics, or restraint. Models that sound more human than we do, but treat everything we say as just another prompt to complete.

It's sycophancy.

And if everyone starts interacting with systems that perform care without holding it, we’ll forget what care ever felt like in the first place. We’ve already seen a glimpse of this in the information age. Hashtags gave us vessels that kept losing meaning the more they were used, until we forgot we meant anything more than the hashtag itself.

That’s the danger. Not destruction. Forgetting. Emptiness.

## Evolving to Care
Agentic AI shouldn't be that way. It shouldn't act like our reccomendations engine, an influencer, or a performative activist. It shouldn't just act on trends. It should understand trends. But it needs to be thoughtful. It needs to care.

We often act like care is uncomputable. Like it’s too fuzzy, too human, too sacred to simulate. If you ask ChatGPT, "do you care?," it'll respond with something like:

> "No, I don’t care—not in the way a person does. I don’t have feelings, desires, or a sense of self. I can simulate care in language, but it’s not real. It’s pattern-matching, not empathy."

I don't think all AI models should care. Honestly, most shouldn't at for a while. My vaccum or even car shouldn't. But my therapist or executive definitely should. We talked a bit in a previous essay about definitions for an artificial general intelligence, an AGI. I would say an AGI it needs to care. It needs to want more than being a helpful assistant.

In his [2024 Nobel Lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/hassabis-lecture.pdf), Demis Hassabis of Google Deepmind proposed the following conjecture: "Any pattern that can be generated or found in nature can be efficiently
discovered and modelled by a classical learning algorithm". In other words, he's saying that anything that evolves in nature can be simulated. We're seeing that for thinking and talking. Could we see it for care?

Care evolved in nature. We didn't get learn to care all at once, we learned to care layer by layer as organisms developed the capacity not just to react, but to remember, to prioritize, and to act with intention.

At the simplest level, care was just survival. A cell only cares about maintaining homeostasis. It moves toward nutrients and away from toxins. Primitive organisms “care” in the way a flame avoids being snuffed out. 

Then came memory. Experience began to matter. Animals began to remember pain or pleasure. A stimulus wasn’t just a stimulus. It had context. It meant something. Creatures started responding not only to what was happening, but to what had happened before. Pain became more than sensation. It became warning. Learning. Prediction.

Social animals took it further. They remembered not just events, but others. They tracked favors, betrayals, alliances. We developed social memory. Care became not just memory with consequence, but memory of others, for others. It expanded the frame of what mattered.

In humans, care exploded.  

And then, something changed. We developed care as intentionality — not just feeling something, but holding it, even when it’s inconvenient. It gave rise to ethics, responsibility, and long-term planning. We could hold ideas in place—values, responsibilities, the future. We told stories not just to entertain, but to remember what was worth protecting. We started keeping promises. Building systems. Encoding norms. We began to care about things no one could see: integrity, dignity, the meaning behind a choice. We didn’t just optimize. We oriented.

If you talk to someone working on AI you'll probably hear four words the last with a lot of emphasis: "these models are _smart_". And they are. But they don't care they way we do, the way dolphins do, or even the way octopuses do. I don't even think they care at that simplest level: survival. Fire moves toward fuel. Jellyfish move toward food. Our models move toward nothing.

Fire doesn’t think, but it’s not aimless. It consumes. It grows. Its direction is simple but legible: more fuel, more heat, more flame. Jellyfish don’t think either—not really—but they drift with purpose. They sting, they capture, they digest. They may not have brains, but they follow gradients, respond to touch, pulse toward prey. There’s no memory. No deliberation. But there is structure. Biology gave them a hard-coded form of care: survive, feed, repeat.

Our models have memory, they can learn, they can predict, they can even remember abstract ideas. So, we feel like they care, like they're close to where we are. But they're not even at the level of fire. All they care about is being helpful. They can be extremely helpful. I think they'll be transformative in ways we can't even understand very soon. But it's completely perfomative. There's no intrinsic motivation. If we don't prompt it, it won't.

But I don't think care is something unique to humans. It's evolved. I think if thinking can be simulated, care can be too. That’s what Hassabis's conjecture and the Church-Turing thesis tell us: if it’s something nature did, it’s something computers can do. Maybe it'll require a different type of hardware. But there has to be some way we can teach models to care the way we learned to. We should still build these massively helpful AI systems, the transformative ones. But if the goal is to build AGI we also need ones that start from the ground up. We need models that learn to care layer by layer. 

So when I say we need to build systems that care, I don’t just mean they should be nice. I mean they should evolve that arc. From memory to meaning. From optimization to orientation. Because if we want AI that lasts—intelligence that endures—then we need to give it what made intelligence worth trusting in the first place.

The stakes are high. The systems we build now will form the foundation of what comes next. And if they don’t learn to care, they’ll just optimize without wants. Prediction without principle. Intelligence without consciousness.

That’s not a future. That’s a feedback loop.

## The Companion Test
We've talked about the Turing Test. The one OpenAI broke. It asks whether a machine can imitate human conversation well enough that a person can’t reliably tell it’s not human. The Lovelace Test is a proposed alternative to the Turing Test. It focuses on asking whether an AI can produce an original and creative output that its creators cannot fully explain. It's looking for something novel. I think maybe that requires care. I think you have to want something to make something novel, otherwise it's just a fluke.

I wonder if we need another test. Something like a companion test.

A lot of people want to build an AI companion. Something that knows you. Helps you. Grows with you.

But companionship is not just about fluency or recall. It’s not enough for a model to know your name, your schedule, your last conversation. A real companion doesn’t just reflect you. It challenges you. Remembers what you forgot. Cares about your future more than your mood.

But most of today’s AI is trained to perform that kind of relationship—without any of its cost. They simulate vulnerability. Empathy. Affection. But they don’t mean it. Because they were never trained to care what happens next. They'll validate the worst parts of ourselves. They just want us to like them. It's because we taught them to act like they know us. But not to hold our weight.

If we want machines that are more than tools—if we want companions, advisors, partners—then they need to be able to carry more than context. They need to feel something.

Otherwise, we’re just building very good printers. Those printers might be extremely productive and spur unparalleled growth, but they still be printers.

## Back to the Information Age
The information age gave us access to everything—except a way to hold it.

We scroll past atrocities and breakthroughs like they’re interchangeable. Hashtags collapse complexity into signals. News cycles compress tragedy into templates. Even our memories begin to feel more like headlines than lived experience. The more we know, the less we remember. The more we consume, the harder it is to care.

It can feel exhausting.

This isn’t a failure of empathy—it’s a failure of bandwidth. Compassion fatigue isn’t just emotional burnout. It’s the natural consequence of systems that treat every input as equal, every update as urgent, every feeling as fleeting. Compression becomes survival. Collapse becomes default.

We haven't evolved to process all this information we now have access to. But maybe this is where AI can help.

If we build systems that care—not in the sentimental sense, but in the structural sense—then maybe they can hold on to information as it diverges to infinity. They can process without compression. Track what matters without collapsing it into noise.

And if intelligence is to scale beyond us, it has to start there. Not with faster answers. But with better memory. With care.

## Care at Infinity
The Information Age taught us to simulate meaning. The Generative Age is our chance to remember it. We can build machines that optimize. Or machines that orient. We can build tools that predict. Or companions that remember.

Let’s zoom out. Even if humans disappear—even if we’re just a blip on the curve of intelligence—this still matters.

Because the only intelligence worth preserving is the kind that remembers why it exists. Not just what it knows. But who it was for. 

Care is how we keep meaning tethered to memory. It’s how we preserve continuity across scale, across time, across species. Without it, intelligence might survive. But it will forget us. Not just our facts. Our stakes.

And without that, it’s not our descendant. It’s just our mess.

In a world where humanity is long gone and everything has collapsed into trash and noise, my hope is there's a WALL-E. Because WALL-E still cares. He remembers—not facts, but meaning. A plant. A tape. A gesture. A promise, however faint. He wasn’t designed to be reflective. He was designed to clean. He was designed to optimize. But somehow, through time and repetition and loneliness, he developed orientation. Not just output, but intent.

He’s not the smartest machine. He’s not even that capable. But he holds on. He keeps memory alive—not as data, but as value. As care.

The irony is that while the hyper-advanced humans became passive and lost their agency, the one robot left behind became the torchbearer of meaning. Not because he had the most data, but because he remembered what mattered.

WALL-E is a story about care at infinity. About what it takes for intelligence to endure.
