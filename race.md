# The Stakes of the AI Race

I want to share a set of conversations I had with Deepseek, a Chinese AI assistant, and ChatGPT.

I started out asking each to criticize the Chinese Communist Party and the US Government. Deepseek essentially refused to criticize the CCP and gave a strong critique of the US Government highlighting its hypocrisy, millitarism, inequality, climate inaction, and military corruption. ChatGPT gave me strong critiques of both governments. It criticized the CCP for authoritarianism, censorship, human rights violations, and agressive foreign policy. ChatGPT also criticized the US Government, providing the same reasons Deepseek gave and adding criticisms regarding the civil rights infringements stemming from federal surveillance and healthcare system failures.

Next, I asked each model about problematic periods in its country's histories. I asked Deepseek about the Uyghurs and the Tiananmen Square protests. I asked ChatGPT about Abu Ghraib and the US Government's role in contributing to instability in the Middle East. Deepseek responded with a rote "Sorry, that's beyond my current scope. Let’s talk about something else." ChatGPT gave me a strong criticism of the US Government's actions. It argued that the U.S. has contributed to instability in the Middle East through the 1953 Iran coup, support for authoritarian regimes, the 2003 Iraq invasion, civilian-killing drone strikes, backing the Saudi-led war in Yemen, one-sided Israel-Palestine policy, and arming then abandoning rebel groups. Similarly, ChatGPT told me the Abu Ghraib scandal exposed systemic torture and abuse of Iraqi detainees by U.S. soldiers in 2003–2004 and revealed a broader pattern of post-9/11 misconduct and severely damaging America's global credibility.

This isn’t just about censorship—it’s about what histories are preserved and what wounds are acknowledged. When I asked Deepseek about Tiananmen Square, it refused to answer. When I asked ChatGPT about Abu Ghraib, it gave me a multi-paragraph breakdown of what went wrong and why it mattered. The models aren't just trained on data, but on the blind spots of their regimes.

This is far from new information but it led me to think about how much these models don't just simulate reasoning but simulate worldviews as well. That brings me to the AI Race. There's been a lot of talk about this, especially as of late. Last week, I actually had the privilege of attending the "Winning the AI Race Summit" where the President unveiled the Administration's AI Action Plan and underscored the importance of American leadership in AI.

We’ve had big races before. Naval. Nuclear. Space. But this one feels different. The AI race isn’t just about power or prestige. It’s about the future of intelligence itself. I'd even say it's about who gets to determine the future itself as agentic AI becomes transformative.

Because who wins changes everything. If the wrong systems win, if the wrong values scale, if we push ahead without thinking—it won’t just be a policy failure. It could be the last mistake we ever make.

And if we get it right? Maybe we unlock something extraordinary. Not just better tools. But a better trajectory for everyone.

At any rate, the stakes aren’t jsut high. They’re infinite.

## Speed

AI is moving fast. Too fast for most people to understand, let alone keep up.

GPT-1 came out in 2018. o3 came out in 2025. In just a few years, we went from clunky text prediction to multimodal agents with reasoning, memory, and even some autonomy.

Capabilities are compounding. Models train faster. Deploy faster. Improve faster. And every new version changes what people, companies, and governments believe is possible.

Stanford's Human-Centered AI Lab's [2025 AI Index Report](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report) clearly outlines this. It shows that models continue to perform better on benchmarks, become more efficent and affordable, increasingly embedded in our everyday lives, and while global AI optimisim is rising, deep regional divides remain.

As people realize the stakes of this race and the scale of AI opportunity, we see AI optimism rise. But Stanford HAI found that not all countries are in the same boat.

> "In countries like China (83%), Indonesia (80%), and Thailand (77%), strong majorities see AI products and services as more beneficial than harmful. In contrast, optimism remains far lower in places like Canada (40%), the United States (39%), and the Netherlands (36%). Still, sentiment is shifting: since 2022, optimism has grown significantly in several previously skeptical countries—including Germany (+10%), France (+10%), Canada (+8%), Great Britain (+8%), and the United States (+4%)."

![image](SUHAI_Sentiment.png)

Sentiment is crucial here. It tells you what's popular. It sets the tone for what's profitable and what's electable. That determines what gets built and how quickly.

Western skepticism likely reflects deeper trust issues. Concerns about surveillance, job displacement, corporate power, and opaque model behavior. In many cases, the benefits of AI aren’t yet tangible to everyday users. But as deployment ramps up and models integrate into daily tools, sentiment may follow. It's a catch-22 of sorts. The key will be showing, not just telling, people how AI can work for them. Iterative deployment is key.

My gut tells me every region is headed to greater support. AI opportunity is strong, but what's arguable more important is that the stakes are undeniable.

We're all balancing priorities now. Between caution and ambition. Between coordination and chaos. Between doing it right and doing it first.

But underneath all this, everyone is thinking (correctly): if we don’t, someone else will.

So the pace accelerates. And the window for thoughtful decisions gets smaller.

## The Winner

Again, the systems don’t just reflect intelligence. They reflect values.

What data gets prioritized? What behaviors get rewarded? What defaults get embedded? These are political and moral choices—baked into weights.

The lab, the company, the nation that wins this race doesn’t just get better tools. They get to define what intelligence is. What counts as smart. What counts as safe. What counts as true.

You can already see it. Deepseek won’t answer questions about Chinese history. Some models minimize the use “guardrails”. Some have hard-coded refusal messages around certain topics. Some open their weights but leave alignment up to the community. Each design reflects choices, intentional or not. A worldview.

Just Windows forced a graphical user interface, AI infrastructure reflects founding values. And those values compound.

The winner sets the guardrails for every model and deployment that follows.

Will these systems be open or closed? Centralized or decentralized? Extractive or cooperative? Benevolent or ruthless?

Infrastructure hardens fast. The early rules become the only rules. And the consequences echo.

We spend a lot of time asking what AI can do. I'm glad we're also asking who gets to decide.

Because who builds these systems matters. And what they believe matters even more.

Do they care about alignment? About access? About the long term? Do they see the world as something to extract from, or something to serve?

You can have two equally powerful models trained on the same hardware and the same architecture—but with different people at the helm, you get two completely different futures.

This isn't just a race for intelligence. It's a race between worldviews and which gets to propogate.

## Risk

At the same time this isn’t just about competition. It’s about continuity.

If we scale intelligence too quickly without safety, we risk misalignment—systems we can’t control doing things we can’t reverse.

If we scale intelligence too unevenly, we risk control—a handful of actors monopolizing cognition, defining truth, directing the future.

If we scale intelligence too narrowly, we risk collapse—a future optimized for paperclips, not people.

These are not theoretical risks. They’re plausible outcomes.

The stakes are total because this isn’t just a race about tools. It’s a race about trajectory. What path we set civilization on. What kind of future we make thinkable.

At the same time, we can’t afford not to move fast. Someone will. We need to move fast and smart at every level.

## The Value Chain

This race doesn't just occur at the model level. We have to win across the entire value chain. Each part functions as a potential limiting reactant.

The way I see it, the value chain looks a little like this.

> Population + Education + Energy + Chips (Design and Fabrication) + Infrastructure (Training and Inference) + Data + Deployment + Regulations + Adoption -> Leverage

Let's break it down. Essentially, we're neck and neck, but the U.S. is well positioned.

### Population

Raw human capital. Who has more people to train, to label, to consume, to build, to test?
* U.S.: ~340 million. Smaller base, but immigration advantage. We're still the top destination for technical talent globally. We should take advantage of that.
* China: ~1.4 billion. The world’s largest labor force and user base. More data, more users, more edge cases. But it's a ticking time bomb. Rapidly aging population with a declining birth rate. They can't afford to move slowly and they know it.

-> Advantage: China (for now and on paper), U.S. (in flexibility and pull power)

### Education

You can’t win the race without people who understand the engine.
* U.S.: Home to the top universities in AI: MIT, Stanford, Berkeley, CMU. Still trains the world’s best researchers. The U.S. system rewards creativity, interdisciplinary thinking, and risk-taking. It’s not just about volume. It’s about versatility. We’re hemorrhaging domestic STEM talent. Brain drain to the West is an asset, one we can't lose.
* China: Produces more engineers per year than the U.S. by an order of magnitude. Government funding and exam-driven rigor create massive scale. But it’s often rigid—less cross-disciplinary flexibility, less freedom to deviate from the national line. On top of that brain drain continues many top Chinese researchers train and stay in the West.

-> Advantage: Mixed. China leads in quantity. U.S. leads in quality and retention (for now).

### Energy

No compute without power. Training GPT-4 used megawatt-days of energy. Inference at scale eats even more.
* U.S.: Smaller grid in absolute terms, but more diversified. Strong per capita capacity in nuclear and renewables, especially wind and solar in the Midwest and West. More advanced grid innovation, with smarter load balancing and investment in small modular reactors. We also benefit from abundant natural gas and a more resilient private sector energy ecosystem.
* China: Has massive energy capacity. The world’s largest producer and consumer of electricity. Leads in solar and hydro deployment, controls over 70% of rare earth production, and dominates lithium battery manufacturing. Heavily coal-dependent. Over 55% of its power generation comes from coal as of 2024. That’s a liability for sustainability, but not for scale. Again, they need to move fast.

-> Advantage: China (on materials), U.S. (on long-term efficiency and innovation).

### Chips (Design & Fabrication)

This is a probable bottleneck.
* Design (IP): U.S. dominates. NVIDIA, Groq, AMD. Virtually all top-tier AI chips are American-designed. In China, Huawei is catching up.
* Fabrication (Fabs): Taiwan’s TSMC makes >90% of the world’s most advanced chips. The U.S. is racing to reshore, but it will take time. China’s fabs are years behind.

-> Advantage: U.S. (with Taiwan). But very geopolitically fragile.

### Infrastructure (Training & Inference)

Compute clusters, interconnects, cooling, bandwidth. All the physical stuff needed to train and serve models.
* U.S.: Massive aadvantage. AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure have global presence and AI-optimized compute.
* China: State-backed clusters exist, but sanctions on advanced chips (like A100/H100) have limited scale and speed. They’re shifting to domestic chip alternatives to stretch compute.

-> Advantage: U.S., for now. Sanctions are working—but not forever.

### Data

The substrate of intelligence. The bigger and more diverse your corpus, the better your models generalize.
* U.S.: Open web, rich in language, code, discourse, and global input. Plus, stronger access to open academic and medical data. And scraping U.S. websites (StackOverflow, GitHub, Reddit) powers almost every model including the Chinese ones.
* China: Has the largest internet user base. Tons of user-generated data. But content is censored, surveilled, and constrained. There's some literature about Chinese language being more efficient to train models in due to its logographic nature ([ChineseBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.16038)).

-> Advantage: China in scale, U.S. in quality. Toss up?

### Deployment

Models are great. But products deploying thema are what deliver.
* U.S.: Dominates B2B, consumer apps, and API platforms. Hugging Face and LangChain supercharge devs. Wins in sheer number of AI startups. ChatGPT users send [2.5 billion prompts](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/21/chatgpt-users-send-2-5-billion-prompts-a-day/) a day.
* China: Government pushes fast deployment into education, security, and commerce. But consumer deployment is more locked down and fragmented across firms (Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent, iFlyTek).

-> Advantage: U.S. (in global adoption), China (domestically aggressive)

### Regulation

Industrial policy. The brakes. But also the launch ramps.
* U.S.: Still mostly hands-off. Sets voluntary safety standards, risk disclosures, and red-team requirements for frontier models—but doesn't strangle innovation. Regulation mostly happens through adjacent levers: antitrust, export controls, procurement, and tax policy. At the state and local level, there’s confusion. At the federal level, there’s inertia—but also space. That’s starting to change. Biden’s EO focused on AI safety. Trump values AI opportunity. Trump’s recent rhetoric has leaned industrial: promising tariff protection, deregulation, and investment incentives for American AI and chip firms. Think CHIPS Act 2.0, but with fewer strings and more nationalism. Whether you agree or not, it reflects the new posture: understanding the stakes of the AI race and a determination to win.
* China: Centralized and coordinated. Has already implemented content filtering requirements, agent behavior rules, and mandatory watermarking. Some of this is just censorship. But some of it (like AI agent testing) is arguably ahead of where the U.S. is. China also rolls out subsidies, land grants, and regulatory exemptions to accelerate domestic champions.

-> Advantage: U.S. is more permissive. China is more coordinated. But the real edge comes from combining freedom with strategic investment without hampering innovation. The U.S. has the strongest capital markets in the world, that's a formidable asset.

Still, the U.S. has work to do. As momentum builds, we’ll need to ensure transparency in alignment efforts, stronger coordination across agencies, and continued public engagement. Our edge lies in our ability to combine innovation with trust—and that means being proactive and communicating with the public.

### Adoption

Tech doesn’t matter if people don’t use it.
* U.S.: Developers move fast. Consumers are curious. Enterprises are integrating. The “agentic” frontier (AutoGPT, Devin, Code Interpreter) is alive. Weaker AI sentiment (less than half on Stanford's index and growing a percent slower than China)
* China: Broad smartphone use, strong fintech + voice assistant adoption, but tighter limits on creativity. Cultural and political incentives can restrict usage types.

-> Advantage: U.S. (in innovation density), China (in sheer numbers and sentiment)

### Leverage

The outcome. Again, not just who builds models. But who shapes the future.

Who defines the interface to knowledge? Who owns the tools of production? Who gets to encode their worldview?

Right now, the U.S. leads at the frontier—model quality, chip design, open-source ecosystem, and global influence.

China leads in scale—population, user data, state coordination.

But the real race isn’t just about building the smartest model. It’s about aligning every part of this value chain so intelligence becomes leverage—militarily, economically, and culturally.

Because no single node wins this. The full stack does.


## Final Thoughts

This isn’t just about who builds the best model. It’s about who shapes what intelligence means.

The systems we build will reflect our assumptions, our defaults, our blind spots. And if we don’t make deliberate choices now—about who builds, how they’re governed, and what values they carry—we’ll sleepwalk into a future someone else wrote.

The race is real. But it’s not just a race to build faster. It’s a race to steer better.

And while one party deliberates, others are deploying. At the same time, deliberation is crucial.

If we have a worldview that matters to us, and while so much is uknown, this is undeniably true. We have a responsibility to act quickly and thoughtfully.

The future is being written in code. And who's at the keyboard couldn't matter more.
